# AI algorithms are grounded in our natural intuitions

## Introduction

In this series of articles, I want to convey some surprising things I've learned from having done AI side projects for over 20 years.  Whether we like it or not, artificial intelligence will play an important role in all of our lives, and it behooves us to have a clear understanding of what it is and what it can do.  I've always hoped and believed that AI would be to the betterment of humanity and that is what AI "enthusiasts" have imagined for the last 20 - 30 years, often motivated by *Star Trek*.  Most of the prominent figures in the research and development of the technology also had this attitude but I think many were stunned by the degree to which the people that ended up owning and controlling and scaling these systems did not have that as a high priority.  Still believing in the cause, I wanted to do my part to combat the hysteria that seems to surround artificial intelligence so that the public makes the right policy choices.  You can't have a proper public discussion of the costs and benefits of AI technology with misinformed public, politicians and business leaders.  This has massive implications for both regulatory and deregulatory legislation and doing nothing is also a choice.  For example, is AI consumption of copyrighted works "Fair Use", since it is transformed into something else?  Should legislation be crafted on the basis of what is practical or enforceable now or based on a general principle and what happens when the technology becomes integrated into our bodies?  There is already the owner/manufacturer responsibility question with self-driving cars but now the same dilemma could apply to almost anything, since AI can be applied to almost anything. 

It is understandable that much of the public thinks that the type of AI depicted in fiction already exists today.  After all, AI exists today that can perform the *acting roles* of many famous robots and androids from science fiction, such as C-3PO from Star Wars and Data from Star Trek.  Most of the acrimony seems to be along two different axis.  On one hand, you have people that don't think AI is real, regarding it as little more than auto-complete with a good press agent.  Then you have the AI-maximalists, who are convinced that artificial general intelligence (AGI) either already exists or is only a sufficient engineering step away.  Then there is the ethical dimension.  Some people are opposed to AI for its impact on jobs, cyber security, inequality and access to power, surveillance and general ease of abuse whereas others are full speed ahead, eager to see their favorite robots from cartoons and movies come to life as well as have an exploitable workforce.  Hopefully, by having a better understanding of how artificial intelligence works, one will be able to have a more nuanced position on these controversies.  One could say that my own position falls on the AI proponent side of the discourse, since I believe it is a "real" manifestation of "intelligence".  However, in order to combat the hype and mysticism that already surrounds AI, I want this article to show that its underlying principles are understandable by ordinary people using only a middle school or high school education.

In everyday conversation, "AI" has become almost synonymous with ChatGPT-like systems: large language models that can write, summarize, and converse.  That association is understandable but it also distorts the discussion.  It encourages the belief that intelligence is primarily about producing fluent language, and it encourages skeptics to dismiss AI as mere imitation because the outputs can be shallow, inconsistent or wrong.  A proper definition of artificial intelligence is more along the lines of computer software that can solve the same types of problems that require intelligence for humans and animals given similar information limitations.  It is tempting to notice the failures of AI at doing some things humans are good at and conclude this is due to lack of intelligence.  Humans solve problems like planning, communicating, inventing tools and coordinating with other humans.  Animals that can move solve problems like navigation, foraging, avoiding predators, and learning which actions lead to harm or reward.  Animals with limited energy stores solve problems like deciding when to search for new resources versus exploit known ones.  The field of artificial intelligence has largely been concerned with isolating these disparate strategies into a unified portfolio of techniques, with the skill being to choose the correct one for a given situation.

## A brief history of AI

Without quibbling too much around the edges, one can think of AI algorithms as originating from three major tracks of study: animal intelligence and biomimicry, direct attempts to model logic and reasoning, and search and optimization algorithms.  The first track includes top down as well as bottom up approaches, inspired by studying natural intelligence from animal learning to brain cells.  Edward Thorndike's work in the early 1900s on animal learning helped formalize the idea that behavior adapts based on consequences and that animals often employ trial and error to achieve a goal.  Pavlov's classical conditioning and later work by B.F. Skinner on operant conditioning emphasized learning through reward and punishment.  These principles were operationalized in modern AI via reinforcement learning strategies; an agent takes an action, receives feedback from the environment and updates its behavior to maximize the desired feedback.  This is how AI can learn to feed the type of videos you like by attempting to maximize its reward in the form of your engagement time.
![[edward_thorndike_cat_boxes.png]]
The 1940s, 50s and 60s saw a lot of pioneering work in biology-inspired machines as well as computation in order to uncover the biological foundations of the animal study results described above.  Here, we find the first attempts to model the computational properties of a neuron by Warren S McCulloch and Walter Pitts.  A major breakthrough that was not sufficiently appreciated at the time, was made by the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt who took the basic ideas of McCulloch and Pitts and extended it to practical applications for perception and sensory processing.  An analytical psychologist by training, Rosenblatt is credited with overseeing the first physical model of a network of interconnected artificial neurons (which he called "Perceptrons") directly in computer circuitry that could learn to recognize basic images and letters in 1960.  In his 1957 proposal to the Office of Naval Research for funding the project, Rosenblatt lays out the ambitions for the system as one that must "be able to recognize  complex patterns of information which are phenomenally similar, or are experientally(sic) related .... able to recognize the 'same' object in different orientations, sizes, colors, or transformations, and against different backgrounds....".  In what was perhaps a bit overselling at the time (but would eventually be vindicated nearly 70 later), the proposal states that it "should be feasible to construct an electronic or electromechanical system which will learn to recognize similarities or identities between patterns of optical, electrical, or tonal information, in a manner which may be closely analogous to the perceptual processes of a biological brain."  Despite the success of Rosenblatt's Mark I Perceptron machine in doing visual processing, his work did not go without some critiques from what would come to be conventional AI proponents.

At the same time as Rosenblatt and others were exploring the capabilities of computers based on simulating neural networks, a competing group of researchers, harkening from the world of logic, mathematics and theoretical computation were approaching the problem from the opposite conceptual end of the spectrum.  They were focused on building machines that could provably solve well-defined problems defined in terms of logic and mathematics.  In what would later be called "expert" systems, inference engines, and probabilistic reasoning systems, the emphases was on understanding the problem as rigorously as possible, and expressing high level human knowledge and reasoning directly in turns of probabilistic and logical notation.  Although this approach has its value and works well in limited well defined situations, it would turn out to be impractical for 'hard', imperfectly described, noisy, practical problems, which would turn out to be problems humans and animals are good at.

Finally, the last major thread of study leading to modern AI is search, multidimensional optimization and statistical techniques.  Straddling the fence between copying biology on one end and formal logical approaches on the other, search and optimization are essential to both techniques at the foundation.  Many problems can be framed as: there is a space of possible actions or solutions and we need to find a good one with limited time.  "Tree" search, heuristic search, randomized search and optimization all fall here.  Even when a system "learns" to solve a problem with logic or simulates an artificial neural network, there is often a computational bottleneck that benefits from efficient search strategies.  Ultimately, modern artificial intelligence systems rely on findings from all 3 branches of study, with state of the art systems being able to take advantage of explicit human knowledge specifications as well as informal, inductive knowledge, feedback from positive or negative responses, abductive reasoning, and sensory information to automatically apply a plausible best approach to a problem.  A key empirical finding from AI research is that you do not need an infinite zoo of techniques to solve most problems.  A small number of frameworks --  search with varying degrees of randomness and prior knowledge, plus pattern recognition and generalization -- covers much of what we call "intelligence".  Again, a key point I want to make is that much of the foundations of these techniques are understandable with only middle school math.  The trick is in understanding the practical implications of the mathematics that is used, which is the subject of world modeling.

## World Modeling

"World modeling" is what connects what you see and expect to see in the external world with something happening in your mind or nervous system.  Where artificial intelligence has fallen short of natural intelligence is in its practical ability to operate in the real world, where trade-offs must be made between prior assumptions and the capacity to learn.  Animals evolved with built-in mental shortcuts that make rapid decision-making possible, called cognitive biases.  Far from a negative thing, cognitive biases define the animal's innate assumptions about the way its universe works.  It is a shared model  that all members of the species use to understand the world in a consistent way that is predictable.  Creating an artificial intelligence that can match the intelligence of biological intelligence benefits by identifying and copying the same tricks and models that biological intelligence uses.  In this context, a "model" is a simplified form of a system that allows you to predict what you care about it.  Ideally, your model will be as small as possible, omitting all unnecessary or irrelevant data.  This benefits you not only by making your model smaller and easier to maintain in your head, but it also potentially makes them more *portable* between different situations.  Arguably, the smallest and simplest model of the world that imposes a minimum requirement for consistency is mathematics.  All mathematics is, at its heart, is a description of the way systems *can* behave but not necessarily *are* behaving (which is the job of science).  A surprising amount of our intuitions can be captured by simple mathematics.  Unfortunately, the deep connection between mathematics and the real world is not traditionally taught to students.  Because of this, people don't understand how the mathematical algorithms used in AI algorithms and computer programs could possibly be attached to things in the real world.  How can the 0's and 1's in a digital computer possibly, "truly" understand the real world?  This is called the "symbol" grounding objection to artificial intelligence being real.

However, I claim that so long as machines are modeling the world the same way as natural intelligence is, you may as well say that it at least understands the world equally well.  There would be no difference to you if you could only observe the effect but not the cause.  Mathematics allows us to model the most basic types of perception that any intelligent being relies on.    For example, consider our visual perception.  Human visional perception is very rich, saturated with detail and meaning.  This would seem to be a reflection of the real world, which is continuous and infinitely detailed (quantum mechanics excepting).  However, our actual awareness and ability to attend to that detail is not continuous and infinitely divisible.

An example that you can experience yourself is how your awareness of objects varies as they shift from the center of your vision to your peripheral vision and finally, to invisibility.  Hold a small object directly in front of you, with outstretched hands, and look at it.  Assuming no vision impairment, you can clearly see the full color and shape and texture of the object to the maximum precision (a rough synonym for "level of detail") allowed by your eyes.  Now, gradually rotate your arm in the same plane while keeping your head and eyes steady facing the same direction that your started.  You will notice that you are able to definitively perceive less and less of the specific properties of the object as it moves farther and farther out of your vision.  If we say that the center of your gaze is at 0 degrees, perhaps if you move the object to 20 degrees to your right, you suddenly are no longer able to fully perceive the shape of the object.  It is not just that the object appears blurry, but rather, the shape of the object becomes increasingly *missing* from your perception.  You may still have an accurate sense of the general height and width and color of the object but you will gradually lose perception of its texture.  At a 45 degree angle, all texture and detail shape are missing from your perception of the object but you can still attend to its average color, size and rough shape.  At 75 degrees, there is no color or texture at all and perhaps just general size and whether the object is horizontally or vertically oriented.  At 80 degrees, you are limited to just rough size and brightness.  At 85 degrees, you only perceive its presence or absence and the edge of your eyelids.

What this example illustrates is the sliding scale of detail provided to your brain by your senses with which to make decisions and act.  A very simple model of the world may only retain information about the presence or absence of objects.  A more detailed model may contain information about the position of those objects.  Then we can increasingly add properties like color and shape and texture and so on.  However, each step of the way, our model still provides useful information about our world.  An animal whose visual system could only detect simple brightness and dark may still have a useful advantage over one that did not if knowing the brightness or dark allowed the animal to make better decisions.  As you add more and more detail to the animal's perception, it can make more distinctions between different types of situations and perhaps make more accurate predictions.  However, a point may be reached where more detail doesn't help you solve your problem any better, and in this case, more detail can be detrimental if it results in more options for making worse decisions.  So part of what must be done with artificial intelligence is to find the right amount of detail to model the world so that its library of techniques can be applied to help you solve problems.  If you can do that, then artificial intelligence is capable of accurately predicting how the real world will behave.

### Simple arithmetic tells us about what we believe about the world (not necessarily how it really is)

Mathematics and logic allow us to *maximize* the predictive power of our model for whatever degree of detail we choose to include in it.  Logic is concerned with models having the precision of true or false for the existence of things or propositions.  Mathematics generalizes to not just true or false but also *intensity* or *strength*.  Objects and forces don't just exist but you can now model the *strength* of their existence.  An animal not only detects whether something is there or not but also how close it is.  This can be further extended to the concept of counting where you could say that the model is reflecting the "strength of presence of multiple independent entities".  This is also useful to animal intelligence since it conveys how many friends or enemies are present.  The simple operation of addition gives our model even more richness, being the simplest model for combining things of the same type where the only detail that matters is how much there is.  Given two different groups A and B, addition is the most basic model for predicting how many objects will be in a combined group containing the members of A and B.  As simple and trivial that it seems, the power of this model, for animals and agents that can utilize it, is that it saves you time from having to recount the members of the combined group so long as you know the number of members of the individual ones.

Mathematics is an iceberg with simple surface interpretations built upon vast meaning below.  In order to build models that can incorporate a large amount of detail from the world, we can build upon simpler ones that only include a small amount of detail but can be combined to build more powerful ones. This ability to combine models is itself a feature of our overall world model, incorporating our assumptions about the *independence* of different objects or events.  Two models or objects or events are "independent" if knowing about either of them, by itself, doesn't tell you anything about what the other is doing or its state.  Independence is a statement about *you* who are building the model rather than the objects being modeled.  The degree to which they *actually are* independent is a feature of the real world but your model can only capture your knowledge and perceptions of it.  One way to model an object's motion on a flat surface is to assume it can move backward and forward independently of its ability to move left or right.  We can then focus on understanding horizontal motion without necessarily considering the vertical motion.  (Indeed, you can do this to understand the flight of an idealized cannon ball that is shot at an angle; to predict its position, you can separately model its vertical motion against gravity and its horizontal motion.  A more complicated model might need to account for wind speed and direction but that detail may not be required for any given problem.)  For some situations, this will be a useful and accurate model of the object's behavior, depending on its manner of locomotion.  And this, at last, leads us to the humble linear function.

### Linear functions

Lines are one of the most basic of models in the world.  They are themselves a generalization of the concept of repetition and combination, but a simpler analogy is the game of tug of war.  Imagine a state faire contest where there are two groups of people competing in a game of tug of war for a prize.  Furthermore, suppose people can pay a fee to submit a prediction of which side will win.  These are called, "predictors" and the participants of the game will be called "players".   Players only win a prize if their side wins.  Predictors only win a prize if they correctly predict which team will win, however, their prize money is split amongst all other predictors.  There will be multiple tug of war games and the fee to become a predictor must be paid for each  game that is predicted.  Also, each game is played by a different combination of teams, so no two teams will play each other twice.   Consider the perspective of a predictor that wants to maximize their earnings.

Most people in this position would probably try their best to account for such things as the age and gender of the players, the team with the tallest players and any hints they might have about which side is likely the most desperate.  These factors would all have to be accounted for in some informal way in order to come up with a prediction from their gut.  One of the main thrusts of this article is that intelligence is being able to predict the winner better than the average person would and that artificial intelligence, with the help of mathematical modeling, would be able to do this well in principle.  The second thrust is how little and simple the mathematics is that can do this, with the right modeling approach.

One might be able to use AI techniques to exceed the performance of humans on this simple task, with the right framing and model.  Where AI maximalists go wrong is by assuming that just because one *can* build a model, it will necessarily be better than informal approaches; this is not necessarily true.  The strength of the mathematical model is that, whatever its shortcomings, it will at least be self-consistent.  Mathematical models don't come out of thin air, they are usually derived from careful observation, experience, and introspection.  Without knowing how complex your model will need to be in order to be "good enough", you may as well start simple.  A pretty simple model would be to estimate the pulling force on one team as merely the number of players on the team.  The model predicts that the team with the most players will be the winner.  In many situations, where all players are fairly similar, simple player count alone may be enough to predict the winning side.  However, this model fails in the cases where either the teams have the same number of players or there is so much variance between individual players that mere comparison of player counts is not sufficient.

In order to make better predictions, we incorporate detail into our model to handle the cases where it fails.  For example, if we knew the masses of each player on each team, then we could estimate a team's pulling force to be the sum of the masses of all its players.  It would then predict the winning team as the one whose total mass was greatest.  We could then look at it from a different perspective.  It is one thing to use the weight of a player to estimate their pulling capacity, but another factor to consider is motivation.  Maybe not all players are equally motivated to apply their full strength.  Maybe some people are only putting an 80% effort whereas someone that really needs the prize money is putting 95% of their maximum effort.  Most people with an middle school education should be familiar with the concept of using percentages to estimate fractions of things.  By dividing the percentage estimate of effort by 100, we can get what mathematicians and scientists call a *weight* which is an estimate of the fraction of capacity that the player is applying.  If we can estimate that force for each player and combine it with their masses, we finally get to the *linear function* x*A + y*B+ ...
![[tug_of_war_example.png]]
You may deride this model for being woefully inadequate but you will be surprised by how well simple linear models can be and many systems in the real world behave like tugs of war.  However, there is a trade-off, where in order to use the linear model, you need more information about each team beyond just the number of players; there is a data collection step where you need an estimate of the mass of each player and their motivation.  At this point, mathematicians can take the problem and run with it, focusing entirely on the problem of finding good estimates for the "motivation" of N independent players given different observations of their success in tug-of-war games.  That can branch into the fields of multi-dimensional optimization, the third line of study leading to artificial intelligence mentioned above.

Here, we finally see how early researchers like McCulloch and Pitts and Frank Rosenblatt realized that neurons can act like linear functions, summing different signals together into a single input which is then compared to an activation threshold (the "bias", in machine learning terms).  If the weighted sum (where each *weight* is defined by properties of the dendrites leading into the neuron) is greater than the activation threshold then "the team of inputs wins" and then the neuron will fire an output.  This would be like the inputs have won the tug of war game against the activation threshold of the cell.  The analogy and model isn't perfect but it has empirically been shown to be useful enough that we have been able to use the same approach to do pattern recognition.  There are many different ways that linear functions can be used to do pattern recognition, but a simple operation called the "dot product" is very easy to understand and can be used to detect simple images.

### Cross-correlation and dot-products - How math can detect image patterns

Hopefully, it is obvious that an image can be constructed from individual dots in a grid that your brain will combine to create a single image gestalt.  Most people should be familiar with this from having looked closely at their phone screens.  Humans can identify an image even in grayscale or black and white based on the arrangement of brightness of the reflected light.  The brightness of light at a particular point in an image can be represented well by a single number.  But what does it mean for an intelligent creature to recognize an image?  How could you model that in a computer?

The task is to model an agent that can recognize an image pattern, guided by our intuitions about what it is like to recognize a pattern in an image.  We will assume the pattern to be recognized is in black and white or grayscale for simplicity; the thought experiment still applies to color images with some minor modifications that don't impact the main example.  One might start with an expectation for what the pattern should look like, which, for technical reasons, we will call the *kernel*, **K**.  Suppose the agent is looking at the kernel.  One simple way to model the process of looking at something is to systematically scan it, taking abreast each part.  It is well established scientifically that humans and animals must constantly scan a scene with their eyes, often subconsciously, in order to get the overall impression of its properties.  For our model, we could use any systematic way to scan the kernel so long as each part is visited exactly one time so we may as well scan the image from top to bottom and left to right.  We want to scan the image at the highest degree of detail, in which case, each piece can be thought of as a small patch of light of a given brightness.  This leads to the question of how to model the brightness of a patch of an image, kernel or otherwise.  It would appear that the sensation of brightness is perceived by humans as a single unitary feeling of intensity, with no constituent parts to this sensation other than perhaps a component of *pain* at the extreme ends.  Pain excepting, we may as well model simple light intensity as a single number between 0 and 1.  Total darkness will correspond to 0 and total brightness will be 1 and all shades of gray in between will receive a number between those two in proportion to its brightness.

We can now model a scan of any grayscale image as being the sequence of light intensity values, recorded as numbers from 0 to 1, that were encountered by a standardized image tracing process.  We will call that sequence of numbers -- which we may as well put in a list of length, say, N -- image A.

image A = \{ I<sub>1</sub>, I<sub>2</sub>, ..., I<sub>N</sub>\} for some intensity values, I<sub>i</sub> that are all between 0 and 1

All object have to be scanned in a standardized way with the same N brightness values in order to be able to compare different images.  Otherwise, we would be comparing patches of brightness that we shouldn't expect to be the same since they would have come from different parts of the objects being compared.  With this in mind, suppose the same procedure was done on the *kernel* image pattern, which we will call image **K** as mentioned above.

The million dollar question is: how does an agent that has an expectation of seeing image **K** perceive another image A?  The feeling of *sameness* should increase as image A appears more and more like with K.  If image A was identical to the kernel then the agent should sense that they are the same.  Since our model involves just two lists of light intensity values, each of length N, then a simple way to capture the "sameness" of the images would be to just compare each intensity value at the same position in each lists.  Because the images were traced the same way, we know we are comparing patches of the base objects that should be in the same relative position in the overall scene.  One approach would be to compare intensity values by simply subtracting them for each corresponding intensity value and sum all N differences together to form a single number, T.  The problem is that defining T this way doesn't match our intuition that similar images should result in a high sensation of sameness and dissimilar images should have a low feeling of "sameness".  The sum of numbers that go into T will give the wrong signal when images need to be the same since it will be 0 for images that are identical but could also be 0 merely due to negative values cancelling positive values, even for images that are dissimilar.  You can use the squared difference in order to avoid the problem of negative number cancellation but you still have the problem that identical images will get small T values instead of larger ones.  How can you create a model for comparing images that returns a large "signal" when two images are similar and smaller signals as the images look increasingly different, the same as our natural feeling of similarity?

#### Centering

AI researchers have discovered a remarkable property of numbers that happens when you reframe each value in an image scan list by subtracting the average intensity of the whole image from each individual intensity value.  The values will no longer range from 0 to 1 but rather from -1 to 1.   This is called, *centering* the light intensity values because the patches with average intensity will be scanned with a value of 0, the "center" of the -1 to 1 range. Lighter shades will be positive and darker shades will be negative.  Note that this is not an idle change; it makes our model reflect real animal eyesight since animals have to be able to recognize the same object in varying levels of ambient light.  This can be seen from the famous checkerboard shading optical illusion: [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checker\_shadow\_illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checker_shadow_illusion).

#### Dot product

Once the intensity values of both image A and the image K have been centered, something almost magical happens when you *multiply* the corresponding centered values from each image and add all N values together to get a new signal value, T2.  T2 has the remarkable property that corresponding patches that happen to lie on the same side of the average (either both below or both above) will combine to *increase* T2 because you are multiplying either two positive numbers or two negative numbers.  Patches on opposite sides of the average will combine to *decrease* T2 because their product will be a negative number.  Thus, the largest possible value of T2 will tend to occur when both images are the same and images that are different will tend to have smaller values of T2, being brought down by the patches with negative intensity products.  This isn't a perfect model since large T2 may also be skewed by outliners but there are other ways to address that (called normalization, to be discussed in future articles).  The sum of products, T2, is so important that it has the special name, *dot product*.  It is called a "product" but the information it returns can be interpreted like a difference because it can be used to compare *any* two patterns, not just images.

#### Cross-correlation

An operation called the *cross-correlation* allows you to compare the image kernel, **K** to any image A that is at least as large.  By overlaying the kernel over a portion of image A and computing the dot product of just the overlapping section, you can build a type of "similarity" map across the whole image A.  The brighter parts of the map will correspond to parts of image A that are most similar in appearance to the kernel, telling you where it is likely to be in the image spatially.  That map *is* the cross-correlation, where each point is derived from the dot-product product of the kernel along image A using a standard overlaying scheme which is often just sequentially offsetting the kernel along all horizontal and vertical spaces that can accommodate the whole shape.  This mathematical object plays a similar role in AI to biological senses, modeling something real in the world just as biological sensors do.  More advanced models are used in state of the art image recognition systems but the cross-correlation is a basic building block of all of them, being able to model an image template.  
The cross-correlation gives you the ability to answer the question: where is this pattern in my visual field?  It allows the agent to perform the basic operation of *semantic* mapping of a pattern with a visual map of where it is located at.  This provides a very basic mechanism where it becomes possible to imagine how predatory intelligent agents that use vision can track a particular type of prey by merely doing something like computing a cross-correlation of an image template for the prey and moving in the direction where it perceives the peaks.

The model isn't perfect because it still doesn't account for the full richness of human vision but the point stands that an abstract model that tracks well with the real world will be able to predict it reasonably well and it just might be well enough to be able to solve real problems. This is indeed the case since deep neural networks called "convolutional neural" networks have taken this basic principle and stretched the analogy so far with hierarchies of layers and abstractions upon abstractions that they are able to perform complex feats of visual understanding and image processing that one might never have predicted originated from the dot-product operation.

This is the beginning of the capacity of a mathematical model to do abstraction.  The value of the cross-correlation is as a higher order direct perception of where one image pattern is in other.  Convolutional neural networks are built with simple kernels that, rather than being whole images, are parts of images such as lines, curves or textures.  The cross-correlation of multiple kernels each detecting a different property of the image can be combined into a layered semantic map which can be used to map out more complex features.

# Conclusion

Hopefully, I was able to get across the idea that artificial intelligence methods are rooted in many of our basic intuitions about the world and it is this reason that it is able to predict and solve problems in it.  Moreover, even simple mathematics can be applied in creative ways to create models that mimic biological systems.  It is not magic.  How good they are is limited to the data they are using to predict with, the data they were trained or developed with, the region of our universe they are applied in, and the particular structural biases and tradeoffs that were used to make the problem tractable.  Indeed, this is a significant source of controversy around the application of AI in real life situations.  A compromise in model complexity or data collection procedures that is deemed acceptable by one group may be unacceptable to another.  An example of this is a machine learning model, such as a face detection system, that performs poorly for certain demographics perhaps not intentionally but due to lack of data, time, money or any combination thereof.

LLMs fall within a special place in the world of AI because not only do we not have a higher order theory for why they work. Also, LLMs have the particular weakness that they do not directly model reality but rather what people have trained them to say about reality.  I will argue that they are not intrinsically flawed but rather have been trained on the wrong data for having an accurate model of the world since it omits the rich sensory perception that accompanies a physical body and our intuitions about the physics of the real world.  But the discussion before should hopefully convince you that it shouldn't be impossible in principle to capture human perceptual intuitions with mathematics and hence replicate some aspect of it with artificial intelligence.

Ultimately, artificial intelligence techniques are really just "intelligence" techniques that happen to be run on a computer.  Many of them can and were initially run by hand, on pencil and paper given that any of them were invented before computers were widespread and practical, like doing manual long division or taking square roots.  In theory, the algorithms could be run by a human, a steam powered computer, animals or a religious procedure using sticks, shells, rocks, seeds, and string.  In future articles, I will share some other AI principles that you can demonstrate and do yourself, such as decision trees, systematic planning strategies, and others.  Hopefully, this will demystify AI, showing that it is important and worthy of serious discussion but also not magic.  There is often an important philosophical principle at the core of AI algorithms that have immediate and practical implications such as the need for diversity of approach (exploration) vs exploiting (gradient descent) a known good enough solution or the fact that a specialized solution will almost always yield the best performance at the cost of generalizability (no free lunch theorem) or the fact that test driven development (TDD) incorporates elements of gradient descent and is thus subject to the same failure modes if not taken into account.  I look forward to sharing more core principles of AI regarding such things as information and multiple worlds, starting with ways of modeling information and how this helps us understand what can be known about a system and how to make the most of what do know to make decisions.
